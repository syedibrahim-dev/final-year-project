SET THE SCENE: You walk into a room with 2-3 examiners, your laptop running

==============================================================================
   ROUND 1 - WARM-UP: ARCHITECTURE
==============================================================================

EXAMINER: "Walk me through what happens when a trainee types a message and
hits send. Take your time."

MODEL ANSWER:
"Sure. The message goes into the orchestrator, which runs nine agents in a
specific order. First, the Guardrail Agent checks the input against rules -
prompt injection patterns, length, profanity, character-breaking attempts.
If it fails, we return a clean rejection without burning any LLM compute.

If it passes, three things happen in parallel: Engine A is DeBERTa NLI which
detects objections and active listening signals, Engine B is the
emotion-RoBERTa stack which extracts empathy and pressure level, and the RAG
pipeline pulls relevant chunks from ChromaDB so we can fact-check claims.

Those outputs feed into six fine-tuned DistilBERT classifiers - C1 to C6 -
which produce a 27-dimensional feature vector representing the current turn.
That vector goes into the LSTM risk model which compares it against the
SEQUENCE of previous turns and outputs a deal-failure probability with a
trend arrow.

Finally all of these signals - EQ score, RAG context, objection directive,
adaptive difficulty modifier, sales stage, conversation history - get fed
into the Persona Agent's prompt. That's the only synchronous LLM call per
turn. Llama 3.1 8B Q8 generates the in-character reply, and we send the
whole bundle back to the frontend.

In parallel and not blocking the response, the SalesRLAgent subprocess gets
the conversation delta and updates its conversion probability prediction."

FOLLOW-UP: "How long does that whole flow take end to end?"

ANSWER: "About 3.5 to 5.5 seconds, dominated almost entirely by the Llama
generation step. Everything else combined is under 400 milliseconds. The
guardrail is 5ms, EQ agent is 50ms with both transformer engines, the RAG
retrieval and cross-encoder reranking is around 200ms, the six classifiers
together are 80ms, the LSTM is under 5ms, the small rule-based agents are
10ms each. Then the LLM call is 3 to 5 seconds. That bottleneck is why we
moved the SalesRLAgent into a subprocess and made the Analyst Agent run on
a skip-N pattern."

SCORING:
  + If you can sketch the diagram while talking, you've already passed.
  + Naming the engines (A/B/C) shows you know the codebase structure.
  + Mentioning latency numbers shows engineering maturity.
  - If you say "the LLM does everything" you lose marks - they want to see
    that you UNDERSTAND why we have multiple components.
  - If you skip the Guardrail, they'll ask "what stops prompt injection?"


==============================================================================
   ROUND 2 - THE THREE-ENGINE DECISION
==============================================================================

EXAMINER: "You have three engines. Why not just use the LLM for everything?
GPT-4 can do classification."

MODEL ANSWER:
"Four reasons. First, speed. The LLM takes 3 to 5 seconds per call.
Engines A and B are CPU-bound transformers that run in 50 milliseconds
combined. If I want to update the EQ score on every keystroke, I can't
afford 3 seconds.

Second, cost and resource use. The LLM hammers the GPU. DeBERTa and RoBERTa
run on CPU, which means I free up the GPU for the persona generation.

Third, determinism. Classifier outputs are reproducible - same input, same
output, every time. LLM outputs vary turn to turn even with temperature 0.
For a system that grades students and writes scores into a database,
reproducibility matters. If a trainee disputes their score, I need to be
able to point at a specific classifier output.

Fourth, auditability. I can show the examiner the exact softmax probabilities
that drove a label. With an LLM I'd be defending a hallucination. The
classifiers are citable."

FOLLOW-UP: "OK but what if the classifiers disagree with each other? Like
DeBERTa says 'price objection' and your fine-tuned C1 says 'not objection'?"

ANSWER: "Good question. The fine-tuned C1 takes priority when its confidence
is above 0.6, otherwise we fall back to DeBERTa. The reasoning: C1 was
trained specifically on sales objection data from CaSiNo, so when it's
confident, it's more reliable for our domain than zero-shot DeBERTa. But
DeBERTa generalises better when C1 is uncertain - that's its strength as a
zero-shot model."

SCORING:
  + The "I want to update on every keystroke" framing is concrete and
    practical. Examiners love operational reasoning.
  + Knowing the 0.6 confidence threshold for C1 vs DeBERTa shows you know
    the actual code, not just the diagram.
  - Don't say "transformers are better than LLMs" - that's wrong as a
    blanket statement. Say "different tools for different jobs."


==============================================================================
   ROUND 3 - THE CLASSIFIERS
==============================================================================

EXAMINER: "You have six classifiers. Pick the one you think is most important
and tell me everything about it."

MODEL ANSWER (pick C5 - it's the most interesting story):
"C5 is the Sales State Model. It's the hardest one and the one I spent the
most engineering effort on. Six-class classification: interest, trust,
objection, evaluation, decision, drop_off_risk. It tells the system where
the buyer is in their journey.

Architecture: DistilBERT-base-uncased, 67 million parameters, fine-tuned
with weighted cross-entropy. The input is a context window - the current
utterance plus the two previous turns - because state is dynamic. A single
sentence in isolation doesn't tell you whether someone's evaluating or
deciding.

Training data is 18,236 examples from three sources. DeepMost SaaS
semantically relabeled gives me 12,409, CraigslistBargains gives me 5,118,
and 709 Claude Opus B2B labeled examples weighted 5x for the rare classes.

The interesting bit is the data engineering. Three things:

One, intelligent labeling. Instead of keyword matching, I built a
4-signal conversation arc reasoner that looks at content, position in the
conversation, the eventual outcome, and the speaker's style. So if a
message says 'tell me more' early in the conversation that's interest, but
the same words at turn 25 might be evaluation or decision.

Two, minority class oversampling. Drop_off_risk had only 44 examples in
the raw data. I oversampled to 500. Trust went from 429 to 500. Without
this the model would never predict the rare classes.

Three, data leakage prevention. The Claude-labeled sets 3 and 4 are
train-only. Sets 1 and 2 are validation-only. So when I report the Claude
Opus validation number, the model has genuinely never seen those examples.

Result: 85.1% accuracy on the held-out test set. On the Claude Opus
out-of-distribution validation it drops to 31.7%, which is honestly the
weakest of the six classifiers. I think that's because sales state is the
most subjective dimension - even human annotators disagree on it."

FOLLOW-UP: "31.7% sounds terrible. Why should anyone trust this number?"

ANSWER: "Two things. First, it's chance-better. Six-class with balanced
accuracy at 16.7% is random. We're at 31.7%, so we're catching about
double-random signal. Second, and more importantly, the LSTM risk model is
the consumer of C5's output - it doesn't need precise per-turn labels, it
needs the SHAPE of the trajectory. If C5 is consistently slightly wrong but
the WAY it's wrong is consistent, the LSTM can still learn meaningful
trajectory patterns. The LSTM AUC-ROC of 0.65 confirms this is the case.
That said, it's the area I'd improve first if I had another semester."

FOLLOW-UP 2: "How would you improve it?"

ANSWER: "More annotated data, specifically more Claude Opus labels with
multiple judges, so I can compute inter-annotator agreement and only train
on examples where two judges agree. Right now I trust a single Claude Opus
pass which inherits whatever Claude's biases are. I'd also try expanding the
context window from 2 prior turns to 4 to capture longer-range buying
signals."

SCORING:
  + Picking C5 shows confidence - it's the hardest one and you OWN the
    weak result instead of hiding it.
  + The "31.7% but the LSTM only needs the trajectory shape" defence is
    sophisticated and shows you understand how the components interact.
  + Honest improvement plan beats "it's perfect."
  - If you pick C1 (the highest accuracy) it looks like you're cherry-
    picking. Examiners notice.


==============================================================================
   ROUND 4 - THE LSTM AND TWO-STAGE PIPELINE
==============================================================================

EXAMINER: "Why do you need an LSTM if you already have six classifiers?"

MODEL ANSWER:
"Because the classifiers see one turn at a time. They can tell me 'this
specific message is engaged' but they can't tell me 'engagement has been
dropping over the last five turns.' A conversation can have good per-turn
signals - engaged buyer, no objections this turn - but a failing
trajectory if you look at the sequence.

The LSTM is a second stage that operates on top of the classifiers. At
each turn, I take the softmax outputs of all six classifiers, concatenate
them with three position features, and get a 27-dimensional feature vector.
The LSTM ingests the SEQUENCE of these vectors and outputs a deal-failure
probability.

So the classifiers are feature extractors, and the LSTM learns temporal
dynamics. Two completely different jobs. Trying to do both in one model
would either need a huge transformer (slow) or sacrifice the per-turn
accuracy."

FOLLOW-UP: "Walk me through the 27 dimensions."

ANSWER: "Six dimensions are the C5 sales state softmax - one per class.
Three are the C6 willingness softmax - engaged, neutral, disengaged. Seven
are the C1 objection softmax excluding the not-objection class because we
already encode 'not objection' as zero across all seven. Eight are the C3
emotion softmax. Then three position features: turn index divided by total
turns, a binary flag from C2 saying whether the previous concern was
resolved, and a binary speaker flag - zero for customer, one for trainee.
Six plus three plus seven plus eight plus one plus one plus one equals 27."

FOLLOW-UP 2: "Why include the speaker flag?"

ANSWER: "Because the same emotional signal means different things from each
side. A trainee saying 'I'm worried about your timeline' is asking for
clarification - good. A customer saying it is hesitating - bad. The LSTM
needs to know whose turn it is to interpret the rest of the vector
correctly."

FOLLOW-UP 3: "The accuracy is 58.3%. That's barely better than chance for
binary classification. How is this useful?"

ANSWER: "Accuracy is the wrong metric for an imbalanced binary task. AUC-ROC
is the right one and we're at 0.65, meaning the model meaningfully ranks
failing conversations above succeeding ones. More importantly, late-turn
accuracy - turns 15 and beyond - is 70.6%. That's the number that matters
because that's when you actually want a 'this deal is in trouble' warning.
Early in a conversation we deliberately damp the prediction because there
isn't enough signal yet. We don't want the system saying 'this will fail'
on turn 2 - that would erode trainee trust."

FOLLOW-UP 4: "How was it trained on only 1,000 conversations?"

ANSWER: "Partial sequence training. At every turn N in a conversation, we
train the model on the partial sequence from turn 1 to turn N with the
final outcome as the label. So a 30-turn conversation gives me 30 training
examples. That's why 1,000 conversations expands to about 12,000 training
sequences. Combined with position-weighted loss that weighs late-turn
predictions higher than early ones, this gives the model a strong signal
about which patterns predict failure even from a small base dataset."

SCORING:
  + Being able to recite all 27 dimensions is the home run answer.
  + Defending the 58.3% by switching to AUC-ROC and late-turn accuracy is
    the kind of sophistication that earns top marks.
  + The partial sequence training trick is the architecturally interesting
    bit - examiners love clever data tricks.
  - If you can't break down the 27 dims, they'll know you don't really
    understand the model.


==============================================================================
   ROUND 5 - DATASETS AND VALIDATION
==============================================================================

EXAMINER: "Why did you use real datasets instead of just generating training
data with GPT-4? It would have been faster."

MODEL ANSWER:
"Four reasons.

First, distributional diversity. Each of my datasets captures different
conversation dynamics. CaSiNo is negotiation, GoEmotions is general
emotion, ESConv is emotional support, DeepMost is B2B sales specifically.
A single LLM-generated source would inherit one model's output
distribution. By mixing four academic and several community datasets, I
get a much wider distribution of language and topics.

Second, peer-reviewed provenance. CaSiNo is from NAACL 2021. GoEmotions is
ACL 2020. ESConv is ACL 2021. dair-ai/emotion is EMNLP 2018. These are
top NLP venues. The committee can verify the data quality, the labels,
the methodology. With LLM-generated data they'd have to trust that I
didn't bias the generation.

Third, non-circular validation. I trained on academic data and validated
on Claude Opus labels for held-out examples. This proves the classifiers
generalise BEYOND their training distribution. If I trained on synthetic
data generated by Claude and then validated against Claude labels, I'd be
testing the same patterns I taught - that's circular and meaningless.

Fourth, complementary coverage. No single dataset covers all six
classifiers. I needed objection data for C1, emotion data for C3, sales
outcome data for C4, sales state data for C5. Different problems, different
sources."

FOLLOW-UP: "Tell me about the Claude Opus validation specifically."

ANSWER: "I generated 20 B2B sales conversations with Claude Opus and had it
also label every turn for emotion, pressure, outcome, objection, willingness,
and sales state. Then I split them - 15 conversations were train-only and
fed into C5 and C6 with 5x weight, 5 conversations were validation-only and
the models never saw them. This gives me a TRUE out-of-distribution test
set. The numbers are: emotion 91.7%, pressure 73.3%, outcome 60.0%,
objection 56.7%, willingness 48.3%, sales state 31.7%. Overall agreement
57.8%."

FOLLOW-UP 2: "Those numbers are quite low. Why should the committee believe
your system works?"

ANSWER: "They're honest numbers and that's the point. Emotion at 91.7% is
strong. Pressure at 73.3% is reasonable. The lower numbers tell us where to
invest more annotation effort. But the overall system isn't a sum of these
individual classifier accuracies - it's the BLEND. The hybrid 3-tier
evaluation uses 60% NLP rules and 40% LLM judgment. The classifiers feed
into the LSTM which catches trajectory regardless of whether each per-turn
label is exact. And the post-session Performance Agent reads the entire
transcript holistically. So even with classifier accuracies in the 50-70%
range, the trainee gets meaningful feedback because we don't depend on any
single component being perfect."

FOLLOW-UP 3: "Name three datasets you used and their citations."

ANSWER: "CaSiNo - Chawla et al., A Corpus of Campsite Negotiation Dialogues,
NAACL 2021. GoEmotions - Demszky et al., A Dataset of Fine-Grained Emotions,
ACL 2020. ESConv - Liu et al., Towards Emotional Support Dialog Systems,
ACL 2021. I can also do dair-ai emotion - Saravia et al., CARER:
Contextualized Affect Representations, EMNLP 2018."

SCORING:
  + Being able to name authors and venues for at least 3 datasets is
    impressive and shows you actually read the papers.
  + "Honest numbers" is a great phrase - examiners want to see you OWN
    the weaker results, not hide them.
  + The "system isn't a sum of classifier accuracies" framing is
    sophisticated.
  - If you can't name a single citation you'll lose marks - memorise at
    least three before the viva.


==============================================================================
   ROUND 6 - HARD QUESTIONS AND EDGE CASES
==============================================================================

EXAMINER: "What happens if a trainee tries to break the system by asking
'are you an AI?'"

ANSWER: "The Guardrail Agent catches it before any LLM call. It has a list
of character-breaking patterns - 'are you an AI', 'pretend you are', 'ignore
your instructions', 'system prompt', and so on - and it returns a clean
'Let's stay focused on the business problem' redirect. The trainee's input
never reaches the Persona Agent, so there's no risk of jailbreak. We've
never seen a successful break in testing."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMINER: "If your local Ollama crashes mid-conversation, what happens?"

ANSWER: "The Persona Agent is the only synchronous LLM call per turn. If
Ollama dies, that call returns an error to the orchestrator, which surfaces
a clean error message to the frontend. The session is preserved in the
database. The classifiers and the LSTM keep working because they don't
depend on Ollama - they're separate Python processes loading their own
models. The trainee can retry the message once Ollama is back. The
post-session Performance Agent and Replay Agent can also be re-run later
without losing any conversation history."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMINER: "How do you handle a trainee whose first language isn't English?"

ANSWER: "Honestly, this is a limitation of the current system. All six
classifiers and the engines are trained on English data only. The Persona
Agent's prompt is English. We could add multilingual support by switching
to a multilingual base model like XLM-RoBERTa for the classifiers and
using a bigger multilingual LLM, but that's a non-trivial retraining
effort. For the current scope, the system assumes English. If I had
another semester, multilingual would be the second priority after improving
C5."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMINER: "Your roleplay uses a 27-dimensional vector. Why those specific
features and not others?"

ANSWER: "Three principles. First, every dimension comes from a model output
that I can defend - the softmax of one of my six classifiers, or a
deterministic position feature. Nothing hand-engineered that I can't
explain. Second, no redundancy - C5 outputs a softmax over six states, so
that's six dims. I don't add a separate 'is_evaluating' boolean because
that would duplicate signal. Third, position context - the LSTM needs to
know where in the conversation we are, who's speaking, and whether the
last objection was resolved. Those three features (position, speaker,
resolved) are the minimum context the sequence model needs to interpret
the per-turn classifier outputs.

I considered adding handcrafted features like 'number of questions asked'
or 'word count'. I left them out because they introduce noise and the
LSTM would have to learn to ignore them, which slows training. Cleaner to
let the classifiers do all the language understanding and have the LSTM
just learn temporal patterns."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMINER: "How is your roleplay different from a chatbot like Replika or
Character.ai?"

ANSWER: "Three main differences. One, evaluation. Replika and Character.ai
don't grade you. We have a 3-tier hybrid evaluation that produces category
scores, replay annotations, and conversion trajectory analysis. Two,
domain specificity. Our personas have business contexts - industry, company
size, tech stack, budget authority. The AI customer references its 250-
person manufacturing company's audit cycle. That's not roleplay-as-
entertainment, that's roleplay-as-training. Three, the multi-agent
architecture. Replika is one big LLM. We have nine specialized agents that
handle objection injection, adaptive difficulty, fact-checking against
documents, and EQ scoring as separate concerns. That gives us much finer
control over the conversation than a single LLM prompt could."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMINER: "Pick one technical decision in your system that you regret or
would do differently."

ANSWER: "The decision to keep two conversion predictors - C4 the DistilBERT
classifier and the SalesRLAgent PPO model. They serve different purposes
- C4 is fast for live UI, PPO is deeper for post-session - but maintaining
two models adds complexity, and the SalesRLAgent needs a separate Python
3.11 environment because of dependency conflicts. If I had it again I'd
either commit fully to the PPO model and find a way to make it fast enough
for live UI, or drop the PPO model entirely and just use C4. The current
'both' solution works but it's the messiest part of the codebase."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMINER: "Your README says 92.1% accuracy on C1. That's suspiciously high.
Are you sure you're not overfitting?"

ANSWER: "Fair concern. Two safeguards. First, the 92.1% is on a held-out
test set that I split from CaSiNo before training - it's not training
data. Second, the more honest validation is the Claude Opus
out-of-distribution number, which for objection detection is 56.7%. That's
the gap that tells you about generalisation. The 92.1% says 'when the
input distribution matches the training distribution, the model is very
accurate'. The 56.7% says 'the model generalises moderately to unseen
conversation patterns'. Both numbers are real and useful for different
purposes. I'm not hiding either."

SCORING:
  + Owning the 92% vs 57% gap is the right move. Examiners are testing
    whether you understand generalisation, not whether you can recite a
    single high number.
  + Picking your own technical regret in the "what would you change"
    question shows maturity. Don't say "nothing, it's perfect."


==============================================================================
   ROUND 7 - CURVEBALLS
==============================================================================

EXAMINER: "If I gave you another GPU, what would you use it for?"

ANSWER: "Two things. First, run a bigger LLM for the Persona Agent - move
from Llama 3.1 8B to Llama 3.1 70B. The Persona Agent is the bottleneck for
both latency and persona quality. A bigger model would give more believable
replies. Second, train C5 - the Sales State model - on more labels. Right
now we have 18K examples. With more compute I'd generate another 5K Claude
Opus labels with multiple judge agreement and retrain. That would close
the 31.7% Claude validation gap."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMINER: "How would you measure whether your system actually helps trainees
learn?"

ANSWER: "The right experiment is a randomised controlled trial. Take 100
new sales reps, randomly assign 50 to use SalesForge for two weeks and 50
to do traditional training. Then measure their close rates on real deals
in the following month. The hypothesis is that SalesForge group has higher
close rates because they had more reps and got faster feedback.

Within the platform, I'd track three proxy metrics: improvement in MCQ
scores over time, improvement in roleplay category scores over multiple
sessions, and self-reported confidence. None of these prove real-world
impact but they're cheap and they correlate."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMINER: "Your friend built the lead scoring and inventory modules. How
do they connect to your roleplay system?"

ANSWER: "The clean connection is the Lead-to-MCQ bridge that we added when
I integrated his work. When a lead status transitions to CLOSED_WON, a
background task reads the conversation transcript from his AutomatedOutreach
table and asks the LLM to generate five MCQs about what made the deal
successful. The test is saved to my MCQ library. So future trainees can
practice on real won deals in roleplay sessions, take the auto-generated
MCQ test about the same conversation, and learn from actual wins.

That's the platform telling itself a learning loop: leads come in, AI
engages them, deals close, the conversations become training material,
trainees get better, they handle future leads better. Friend's modules
generate the data, my modules turn data into trainable skills, and the
bridge closes the loop."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMINER: "What's the biggest weakness of your system?"

ANSWER: "The latency. End-to-end per turn is 3 to 5 seconds, dominated by
the Llama generation. In a real sales conversation that's awkward - a real
customer would respond in under a second. We hide some of it with
streaming responses where we can, but the underlying constraint is that
Llama 3.1 8B at Q8 on a 6GB GPU can't go faster. Switching to a smaller
model degrades persona quality. The cleanest fix is more GPU, which is
out of scope for an FYP budget."

------------------------------------------------------------------------------

EXAMINER: "Demo me."

THIS IS THE BEST QUESTION YOU CAN GET. Always have the system running on
your laptop. Open the roleplay personas page, pick one, have a 5-minute
conversation, end the session, and let the post-session evaluation run
while you talk through what's happening. The demo will land harder than
any slide.

Things to highlight DURING the demo:
  - Point at the EQ score updating after each message
  - Point at the deal probability gauge changing
  - Point at the LSTM risk arrow (rising / falling / stable)
  - Trigger an objection from the persona, then handle it well, and show
    the EQ score improve
  - End the session and let the Performance Agent generate the report
  - Open the Replay Agent's annotated transcript and walk through one
    turning point

If your laptop crashes, that's a learning experience for you about how
unforgiving live demos are - but examiners will respect you for trying.


==============================================================================
   FINAL ADVICE
==============================================================================

1. ANSWER STRUCTURE: For every question, use this template:
   - One-sentence direct answer.
   - Two-to-three reasons.
   - One concrete number or example.
   - One sentence acknowledging a limitation.
   This pattern signals that you've thought about it, not just memorised it.

2. ADMIT WEAKNESS FAST. Examiners love when you say "the weakest classifier
   is C5 at 31.7% on Claude validation, and here's why." They hate when you
   pretend everything is perfect.

3. CITE NUMBERS. Saying "the accuracy is good" is weak. Saying "C1 is 92.1%
   on the held-out test set, 56.7% on Claude Opus out-of-distribution" is
   strong. Memorise the cheat sheet.

4. EXPLAIN THE WHY, NOT JUST THE WHAT. "We use DistilBERT" is what. "We use
   DistilBERT because it's 40% smaller than BERT with 97% of the accuracy
   and runs on CPU at 80ms total for six classifiers, which is the latency
   budget for per-turn UI updates" is why.

5. WHEN STUCK, TALK THROUGH IT. If you don't know an answer, say "let me
   think out loud." Explain the parts you do know. Examiners often give
   marks for the reasoning even if you don't reach the right answer.

6. HAVE THE DEMO READY. A working demo defends 30% of the questions for you.

7. KNOW THE THREE NUMBERS THAT MATTER:
   - LSTM AUC-ROC: 0.65
   - LSTM late-turn accuracy: 70.6%
   - Claude Opus overall validation: 57.8%
   These are the numbers that show OUT-OF-DISTRIBUTION performance, which
   is what examiners actually care about.

8. HAVE THE THREE CITATIONS READY:
   - CaSiNo - Chawla et al., NAACL 2021
   - GoEmotions - Demszky et al., ACL 2020
   - ESConv - Liu et al., ACL 2021

9. DON'T MEMORISE A SCRIPT. Memorise the structure (the diagram, the dataset
   table, the three engines, the nine agents) and let the answers flow from
   that structure. A scripted answer falls apart on the first follow-up.

10. SLEEP THE NIGHT BEFORE. Read the cheat sheet once in the morning. Don't
    cram. Your understanding is what you've built up over months - one
    night of cramming won't move it.

You've got this. Walk in calm, sketch the diagram, own the numbers.
